This page summarizes the paper. It is written for people working on Bitcoin policy in states with Democratic legislatures, where few bills have been tried. The figures and state lists are as of April 2026, when the paper was published. For what has happened since, see the record from other states in the 2027 agenda.

Part I. Four ways to frame itBitcoin is not a partisan issue

SECTION 1. Wealth inequality. When new money is created, it reaches asset holders before it reaches wage earners, an effect named for the economist Richard Cantillon. Sound money protects the purchasing power of wages. The top 1 percent held 35 percent of wealth in 1971 and 50 percent in 2024.

SECTION 2. Financial inclusion. About 1.4 billion adults worldwide have no bank account. Bitcoin requires no account, credit score or permission. A remittance over the Lightning network costs $2 to $4, against about $19.50 by traditional means.

SECTION 3. Transparency. Every transaction on Bitcoin’s ledger can be verified by anyone, which makes it the most auditable monetary system there is.

SECTION 4. Energy. 52.4 percent of mining runs on sustainable energy, and coal’s share fell from 36.6 percent in 2022 to 8.9 percent in 2025. Miners use energy that would otherwise be flared or curtailed.

The opening argument: what changed after the dollar’s link to gold ended in 1971. These numbers are about what happens when money can be created without limit.

Part IV. From first contact to committee hearing

SECTION 5. Find the right sponsors. Committee assignment matters more than enthusiasm. A Democratic primary sponsor with a Republican co-sponsor shows the bill is nonpartisan. Look for members of finance committees and members who are curious about technology.

SECTION 6. Build a coalition beyond Bitcoin. Work with civil liberties groups on private key protection, small business associations on the de minimis exemption, and economic development organizations on sandboxes and charters. A bill should have more supporters from outside Bitcoin than from inside it.

SECTION 7. Testify well. Establish who you are, state your position, name the problem, present the solution with precedent, answer the likely objections and close with what you are asking for. Do not read from a script, and keep it under five minutes.

SECTION 8. Work with staff. Committee staff and legislative counsel shape bills. Give them one-pagers, fiscal analyses and section-by-section summaries, and make their job easier.

SECTION 9. Lead with data. Before you publish anything, check that it leads with data, names a real problem, offers a concrete solution, and would strike a skeptic as reasonable. If it fails any of the four, revise it.

Part V. The objections you will hear

Bitcoin wastes too much energy.

Mining is 52.4 percent sustainable, and coal fell from 36.6 percent to 8.9 percent in three years. Mining uses 0.7 to 0.8 percent of the world’s electricity and increasingly runs on energy that would otherwise be wasted.

It is only used by criminals.

Illicit activity was 0.34 percent of on-chain volume in 2024, according to Chainalysis. The public ledger makes Bitcoin a poor tool for crime. The Colonial Pipeline ransom was traced and recovered within days.

It is too volatile for serious policy.

Volatility is falling as the market matures. A de minimis exemption, a study commission and a private key bill do not require the state to hold any bitcoin, so the policy can be judged apart from the price.

Only wealthy speculators benefit.

74 percent of Bitcoin owners hold less than 0.01 BTC, and the median amount people have put into digital assets is about $620. The de minimis exemption is aimed at small everyday transactions.

We should wait for federal guidance.

Wyoming did not wait, and its laws became the national model. States that act early help shape the federal framework.

We do not have the expertise.

That is the argument for a study commission, which commits the state to learning before it commits to policy.
