Bitcoin 101A primer for legislators and staff on what Bitcoin is, how it works, and the questions committees ask most

Updated November 17, 2026
About ten minutes to read

Part I. What Bitcoin is

SECTION 1. Digital money. Bitcoin is a payment network, like Visa, and a form of money, like dollars or gold. It was created in 2009 and lets people send and receive value directly, without a bank in the middle. The network has settled more than $30 trillion in transactions over its lifetime.

SECTION 2. No central operator. No company or government runs Bitcoin. Every transaction can be checked on a public ledger kept by thousands of independent computers around the world.

SECTION 3. A fixed supply. The network’s software limits the supply to 21 million coins, and that limit cannot change unless the people running the network agree to change it. This is why Bitcoin is often compared to gold.

A note on capitals. We write Bitcoin with a capital B for the network and bitcoin in lowercase for the currency unit, the way one writes the Internet and internet traffic.

Part II. How it works

SECTION 4. Definitions. As used in this primer:

  • (1) “Wallet” means software that stores the credentials for your bitcoin. It works like a bank account that you control directly.
  • (2) “Private key” means the secret code that proves ownership and authorizes a transaction. It is like a PIN, secured by cryptography.
  • (3) “Blockchain” means the public record of every transaction. It is permanent and cannot be quietly altered.
  • (4) “Mining” means the computing work that verifies transactions, secures the network and issues new bitcoin.

SECTION 5. A transaction, step by step.

  • (a) Alice signs the transaction with her private key.
  • (b) The transaction is broadcast to the network.
  • (c) Miners verify that it is valid.
  • (d) It is recorded on the blockchain permanently.
  • (e) Bob receives the bitcoin, often within minutes.

For legislators. Because the ledger is public and permanent, Bitcoin is easier to trace than cash. Law enforcement agencies have solved major cases because the people involved used it.

Part III. Mining and energy

SECTION 6. Security. The cost of the computing work is what makes it uneconomic to attack or rewrite the network. Mining uses roughly 0.7 to 0.8 percent of the world’s electricity, according to Cambridge data, and secures hundreds of billions of dollars in value.

SECTION 7. Energy mix. In its April 2025 report the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance found that 52.4 percent of mining ran on sustainable energy, 42.6 percent from renewables and 9.8 percent from nuclear. Earlier estimates were near 38 percent. Coal’s share has fallen substantially over the same period.

SECTION 8. The grid. In Texas, the grid operator uses miners as flexible load. They shut down during peak demand and absorb cheap power during surpluses, and some run on gas that would otherwise be flared.

Source: Cambridge Digital Mining Industry Report, April 2025.

Part IV. Questions committees ask

Is Bitcoin mainly used for crime?

Illicit activity accounts for less than 1 percent of Bitcoin transactions, and the dollar remains the main currency for money laundering. The public ledger helps investigators because it leaves a permanent record.

Does Bitcoin waste energy?

More than half of mining runs on sustainable energy, per the Cambridge figures above. A meaningful share uses energy that would otherwise go unused, such as flared gas and curtailed wind and solar.

Is Bitcoin too volatile?

Bitcoin is volatile, and it has become less so as the market has grown. It has produced positive returns over every completed four-year period in its history, though returns have moderated. BlackRock and Fidelity now offer Bitcoin products.

Part V. Why states are acting

SECTION 9. Business location. Wyoming’s blockchain laws drew Kraken’s bank subsidiary, BitGo and dozens of financial technology firms to a state of 588,000 people. Rhode Island has roughly twice that population.

SECTION 10. Tax clarity. Every purchase made with bitcoin is a reportable capital gains event in Rhode Island, a $5 coffee included. RIBPI estimated that the 2026 exemption bill would have cost the state $500,000 to $2.5 million a year, under 0.04 percent of general revenue.

SECTION 11. Digital property rights. Wyoming’s 2023 private key law, which passed its Senate 31–0, is the model states use to protect a person’s control of their own keys.

Part VI. What Rhode Island can do

  • Educate the study commissionS.2198 Sub AThe Senate created a ten-member commission on June 10, 2026. Its interim report is due January 5, 2027.
  • Protect private keysReturning in 2027No compelled disclosure of a private key when a public key will do. Law in Wyoming and Utah.
  • Exempt small bitcoin paymentsRevised for 2027Takes everyday purchases out of state income tax, as federal law already does for small foreign-currency purchases.
  • Hold unclaimed bitcoin as bitcoinNew for 2027So an owner who comes forward gets the asset back. Enacted in Virginia, Maine, Utah and Alabama in 2026.
