The Senate created a study commission on blockchain and cryptocurrency. Every other bill RIBPI tracked was held for further study and expired when the session ended. A bill held for further study does not carry over. It has to be introduced again to be considered in 2027.

Adopted

S.2198 Sub A. Blockchain and cryptocurrency study commission. Creates a ten-member Special Legislative Commission to Study Blockchain and Cryptocurrency: three senators, the Secretary of Commerce, the Director of the Department of Business Regulation, and five public members. Substitute A converted the measure from a joint commission to a Senate commission, so the Senate vote was the final action required. The commission is expected to begin meeting soon.

RIBPI’s position: strong support. Wyoming set up a blockchain task force in 2018 and has enacted more than thirty related laws since. Rhode Island will now do the same work with senators, executive-branch officials, academics and people from finance and securities law in the room. RIBPI thanks Sen. DiPalma, the Senate Artificial Intelligence & Emerging Technologies Committee and the Senate.

    S.2021. Bitcoin tax de minimis exemption. Would have exempted Bitcoin transactions of up to $5,000 a month or $20,000 a year per taxpayer from state income tax, as a one-year pilot ending January 1, 2028.

    RIBPI’s position: strong support. Every purchase made with bitcoin is a taxable sale of property, so a $5 coffee requires a gain or loss calculation. JPMorgan Chase Institute research puts the median amount sent to digital asset accounts at about $620, which means the people affected are ordinary Rhode Islanders and not large investors. RIBPI estimated the cost at $500,000 to $2.5 million a year. The Division of Taxation’s June 8 letter took no position and raised administration concerns, which the 2027 draft is written to answer.

      H.7957 and S.2196. Private Key Protection Act. Would have barred courts and agencies from compelling a person to disclose a private cryptographic key in proceedings about digital assets or identities, unless a public key was unavailable or would not give access to the information needed.

      RIBPI’s position: strong support. A private key is a master key to every asset, identity and account tied to it. Ordering someone to hand it over for one transaction is like demanding the deed to a house when the court needs only the address. The bill keeps every existing court and law enforcement power and says the public key should be used when it is enough. Wyoming enacted the same rule in 2023 by 31–0 in its Senate and 55–6 in its House.

        H.7413. Rhode Island Economic Growth Blockchain Act. Would have set up a regulatory sandbox and public-private partnerships for blockchain companies, with temporary regulatory exemptions for testing financial products, and would have created special purpose depository institutions required to hold 100 percent liquid reserves.

        RIBPI’s position: strong support in 2026. Wyoming and Nebraska are the only states with special purpose depository charters. For 2027 we will bring the charter and sandbox questions to the study commission before asking for a bill.

          H.7843. Rhode Island Decentralized Autonomous Organization Act. Would have let decentralized autonomous organizations form as limited liability companies in Rhode Island, with governance through smart contracts, annual reporting to the Department of Business Regulation, a regulatory sandbox for pilot projects, and capital formation through federally compliant private placements.

          RIBPI’s position: support with amendments. Wyoming, Vermont, Tennessee and Utah have DAO statutes. The bill as introduced had no statutory definition of “DAO” or “smart contract,” did not say how it relates to existing LLC law, left the sandbox undefined, gave the Department no time to prepare, and did not address smart contract failures or require organizers to be identified. RIBPI offered amendment language on each point.

            H.7955 and S.2648. Virtual currency kiosk definitions. Would have widened the definitions of kiosk “operator” and “transaction” in the 2025 kiosk law to reach businesses that use an app to send customers to pay in person through a clerk in place of a kiosk machine.

            RIBPI’s position: concerns. We support closing that gap. As drafted, the phrase “facilitates or enables” could also reach software developers, retail chains, payment processors and non-custodial services that look nothing like the businesses the bill is aimed at, and several key terms were left undefined. Every business newly covered would have taken on the full compliance requirements of chapter 19-14.3 on the day of passage. RIBPI asked Senate Commerce to hold S.2648 until the study commission existed and had built a record. Both bills were held, and the commission was created on June 10.
